Wheel-throwing vs. hand-building

Wheel-throwing

You center a ball of clay on a spinning wheel and pull it up into a round shape, like a cup, bowl or vase. It’s fast once you have the feel for it, but centering takes weeks of practice, so expect your first pots to collapse.

Hand-building

You shape clay with your hands and simple tools using pinch pots, coils or flat slabs. It needs no wheel and is slower, which makes it a forgiving place to start and good for shapes that are not round.

Glazing

A glaze is a thin layer of glass that melts onto the pot in the kiln. It can be glossy, matte or speckled depending on its recipe and how hot the kiln gets. Brush it on, dip the whole pot, or pour it — but leave the foot dry so the piece does not fuse to the shelf. Test tiles first; the colour in the bucket is rarely the colour that comes out.

Drawing & painting styles

Ink

Brush and nib ink reward a confident line; a single stroke cannot be corrected, so plan the shape before you touch the paper.

Digital

A tablet mimics charcoal and ink without the dust, and layers let you try a dozen versions of the same vase before committing.

Charcoal

Vine and compressed charcoal smudge and lift with an eraser, which makes them forgiving for quick studies of form and shadow.

Artists worth knowing

Adelaide Alsop Robineau

A Syracuse porcelain artist of the early 1900s known for intricately carved and glazed vases. Her work is in the Everson Museum of Art, so you can see it in person.

Lucie Rie

An Austrian-born British potter famous for thin, elegant porcelain bowls and vases with subtle, textured glazes. A good example of how simple a beautiful shape can be.

Beatrice Wood

An American ceramic artist known for her shimmering luster glazes. She began working with clay in her forties and kept making pottery for decades, which is encouraging if you feel like a late starter.
